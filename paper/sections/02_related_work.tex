\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Predictive-maintenance baselines}

Predictive-maintenance models often begin with supervised classification, reconstruction error, distance-based anomaly detection, or streaming thresholds. Isolation Forest isolates anomalies by random partitioning and remains a standard unsupervised baseline \citep{Liu2008IsolationForest}; autoencoders detect anomalies through reconstruction error under a learned nonlinear manifold \citep{Sakurada2014Autoencoder}; offline and online change-point methods target shifts in the generating distribution rather than pointwise extremity \citep{Truong2020ChangePoint}; and conformal prediction supplies distribution-free validity guarantees \citep{Vovk2005Conformal} that extend to sequential anomaly detection \citep{Laxhammar2015ConformalAnomaly}. All five appear as baselines in the benchmark reported here. Their common output is a timestamped score rather than an episode-level maintenance decision, which is the gap this paper measures.

\subsection{Dependence-aware extreme-value theory}

Classical peaks-over-threshold modelling fits a generalised Pareto tail to exceedances over a high threshold \citep{DavisonSmith1990Exceedances}, and the standard treatment covers threshold choice, return levels, and nonstationary extensions in which the tail parameters vary with covariates or time \citep{Coles2001ExtremeValues}. Independence of exceedances is the assumption that industrial telemetry violates most obviously. Leadbetter's extremal index quantifies that violation as the reciprocal of the mean cluster size under local dependence \citep{Leadbetter1983Extremal}, Ferro and Segers estimate it from inter-exceedance times \citep{FerroSegers2003Clusters}, and the K-gaps estimator conditions on a declustering gap chosen from the data \citep{SuvegesDavison2010KGap}. EVT-based streaming detectors such as SPOT adapt anomaly thresholds online using the same tail machinery \citep{Siffer2017SPOT}; they address threshold calibration, but not operating-regime mixture, duplicate alarms per failure, or the distinction between clustered observations and independent maintenance events.

In dynamical systems, hitting-time statistics connect returns to target neighborhoods with extreme-value laws \citep{Freitas2008Hitting}. Periodic points can generate an extremal index smaller than one, giving a mathematical route from recurrence to clustered extremes \citep{Freitas2012Periodicity}. The finite-sample industrial setting is less clean: noise may make deterministic recurrence appear closer to independence, and observed clustering can also arise from smoothing, missingness, persistence, or regime mixture \citep{Faranda2013Noise}. Recent multivariate work extends dynamical EVT to cross-sectional dependence among vector-valued observables \citep{Aimino2024Multivariate}, which motivates the lag-pattern diagnostics used here.

\subsection{Event-level evaluation and alarm burden}

Pointwise precision and recall reward a detector for flagging many samples inside one long anomaly, so time-series evaluation requires range-aware definitions \citep{Tatbul2018PrecisionRecall}. The point-adjust convention widely used in that literature is now known to inflate scores so severely that random scores can appear competitive \citep{Kim2022RigorousEvaluation}. This paper evaluates at the episode level for that reason, and reports the degenerate case explicitly: after alarm merging, a permanently-on detector attains precision and recall of one, so warning exposure is reported alongside episode counts throughout.

The operational cost of that burden is the subject of established alarm-management practice. Both the process-industry standard and the corresponding engineering guide specify alarm rates an operator can absorb and treat alarm rationalisation as a design activity rather than a post-hoc filter \citep{ISA182AlarmManagement, EEMUA191AlarmSystems}. The rates reported here, between 9 and 105 false alarms per operating day, are one to two orders of magnitude above those recommendations, which is the basis for the operational conclusion in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Transferability and domain shift}

Whether a detector calibrated on one machine transfers to another is rarely tested on the datasets used above, because most report a single asset. MetroPT and MetroPT2 are the exception in this collection: they cover comparable compressor subsystems and permit a genuine cross-dataset experiment \citep{Veloso2022MetroPT, MetroPT2Zenodo2023}. Section~\ref{sec:transfer} uses that pair to separate schema compatibility from transferability, and reports the frozen and recalibrated variants separately rather than reporting a single transfer number.

The industrial datasets used by the repository are public condition-monitoring resources with different independence units and labels. MetroPT is an air-production-unit telemetry dataset for predictive maintenance \citep{Veloso2022MetroPT}. MetroPT2 is a replication-style benchmark released through Zenodo \citep{MetroPT2Zenodo2023}. SCANIA Component X is a fleet-level multivariate time-series dataset with operational histories and repair records for an anonymized component \citep{Kharazian2025Scania}. UCI Hydraulic Systems provides laboratory hydraulic test-rig cycles with component-condition targets \citep{Helwig2018HydraulicSystems}. UCI SECOM provides semiconductor process measurements with pass/fail yield labels \citep{McCann2008SECOM}. These datasets broaden the empirical base, but they do not remove the need for estimand-specific accounting: one long compressor fault interval, one truck repair history, one hydraulic load cycle, and one wafer-level yield example are not interchangeable statistical units. The methodological gap addressed here is the absence of a reproducible layer that connects dynamical extreme diagnostics to alarm episodes, tabular condition diagnostics, sensitivity analyses, and claim constraints.
