\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Simulation validity and estimator stability}

The first research question asks whether the extremal-index diagnostics are stable enough to interpret. The high-replication simulation artifact covers fourteen process families with 500 replications per configured cell, and a focused coverage artifact uses 1000 replications on a preregistered subset. The six estimators of Section~\ref{sec:simulation} are compared against theoretical references where a closed form exists and numerical references otherwise, with unestablished configurations excluded from every performance quantity. Table~\ref{tab:estimator-main-summary} reports one representative process per clustering regime; the full grid and the reference-availability audit are in the supplement.

The central result is that the estimator ranking \emph{inverts} between regimes. For the independent process, where \(\theta = 1\), runs and K-gaps record interval coverage of \(0.40\) and \(0.39\) against a nominal \(0.95\), with biases of \(-0.133\) and \(-0.117\). For the periodic target, where \(\theta = 1/2\), the same two estimators are the least biased of the six, at \(-0.010\) and \(+0.003\), with coverage of \(0.844\) and \(0.850\). A reader who saw only the near-independent rows would conclude against precisely the estimators that work once extremes actually cluster.

The apparent superiority of Ferro--Segers and disjoint blocks at \(\theta = 1\) requires care. Both attain coverage of \(1.000\) there, but with boundary-clipping probabilities between \(0.50\) and \(0.70\): their intervals contain the truth largely because the estimate saturates at the admissible boundary \(\theta = 1\), not because they are calibrated. Figure~\ref{fig:estimator-failure} reports the clipping share beside the non-estimability share for this reason, and coverage at \(\theta = 1\) should not be read without it. The undercoverage of runs and K-gaps has the same origin: at the boundary the estimate can only err downwards, so a symmetric percentile interval misses systematically. Coverage comparisons at \(\theta = 1\) are structurally distorted for every estimator, which is a further reason to treat the near-independent grid as bounded evidence.

The no-declustering control behaves exactly as intended, with zero bias at \(\theta = 1\) and bias \(+0.500\) and \(+0.728\) in the two clustered rows, confirming that the comparison is measuring extremal clustering rather than an artifact of the harness.

\subsection{Alarm conversion and failed-detection regions}

The second research question asks whether declustering and alarm merging reduce duplicate alarms while preserving event recall. In the registered threshold grid, event recall was zero at all reported quantiles, with false-alarm burdens between 133.92 and 168.48 alarm episodes per operating day. This is a failed-detection region under the current observable, threshold grid, and matching rule; it shows that alarm conversion can destroy utility. The full threshold grid is reported in the supplement rather than as a main-text figure because a zero-recall line plot would add no scientific information.

Across all registered combinations of alarm merge gap and post-onset matching tolerance, event recall, precision, and \(F_1\) remained zero. Relaxing post-onset tolerance did not alter the result, so the full grid is reported in the supplement rather than as a main-text sensitivity figure.

\subsection{Held-out event-level method comparison}

The third and fourth research questions ask whether the registered robust extreme-score diagnostic adds operational value after episode conversion, and why empirical failures occur. Table~\ref{tab:event-level-results} gives the held-out event-level rows for MetroPT and MetroPT2. Each dataset contains one labelled test event in the registered split. Both events are detected by the registered score, but the detection is operationally weak: MetroPT produces 2631 predicted alarm episodes with precision \(0.00038\), and MetroPT2 produces 852 predicted alarm episodes with precision \(0.00117\). The median lead times are not persuasive by themselves because they are paired with extreme false-alarm burdens.

Alarm burden must be read on two axes that can disagree. Episode counts measure how often an operator is interrupted; time under warning measures how long the system is held in an alarmed state. On MetroPT the engineering threshold raises fewer episodes than the registered score (1551 against 2631) while holding the system under warning for 75.1\% of the test period against the registered score's 0.12\%. A detector that merges its exceedances into few long alarms therefore looks better on episode counts and far worse on exposure. Because episode-level precision and recall are computed after merging, a permanently-on detector attains a recall and precision of one with no unmatched episodes to count as false alarms; exposure is the only reported quantity that exposes it. Every alarm-burden comparison in this paper is therefore reported with both axes, and the supplementary benchmark flags any method whose warning exposure exceeds half the test period.

Table~\ref{tab:event-baseline-comparison} compares the registered score with a compact event-level baseline set under the same held-out event policy. The table keeps the best simple rule class, a classical EVT baseline, an anomaly baseline, the registered recurrence score, and the two target-region methods. The full baseline family remains in the generated CSV artifact.

\subsection{Target-region heterogeneity across MetroPT and MetroPT2}

The strongest empirical divergence is the failure-prototype target region. It materially reduces alarm burden in MetroPT2 but fails to detect the held-out MetroPT event. Table~\ref{tab:target-region-transferability} reports the within-dataset and cross-dataset target-region comparison for failure-prototype and rare-state regions. This instability means the two compressor datasets cannot be treated as equivalent evidence for one transferable recurrence region.

\subsection{Supplementary non-event stress tests}

Hydraulic Systems, SCANIA Component X, and SECOM are reported separately in Table~\ref{tab:non-event-results} because their independent units are cycles, vehicles, and wafers rather than compressor failure episodes. All three have \(F_1=0\) under the conservative high-quantile diagnostic. These are not hidden failures. They are evidence that a simple extreme-score adapter does not transfer across laboratory cycle states, vehicle-level repair risk, and wafer-level yield labels without changing the estimand and evaluation design.
