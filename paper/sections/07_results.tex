\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Simulation validity and estimator stability}

The first research question asks whether the extremal-index diagnostics are stable enough to interpret. The high-replication simulation artifact covers fourteen process families with 500 replications per configured cell, and a focused coverage artifact uses 1000 replications on a pre-specified subset. The six estimators of Section~\ref{sec:simulation} are compared against theoretical references where a closed form exists and numerical references otherwise, with unestablished configurations excluded from every performance quantity. Table~\ref{tab:estimator-main-summary} reports one representative process per clustering regime; the full grid and the reference-availability audit are in the supplement.

The central result is that the estimator ranking \emph{inverts} between regimes, and it shows in bias before it shows anywhere else. For the independent process, where \(\theta = 1\), runs and K-gaps carry biases of \(-0.133\) and \(-0.117\), the largest of the six. For the periodic target, where \(\theta = 1/2\), the same two estimators are the least biased, at \(-0.010\) and \(+0.003\). A reader who saw only the near-independent rows would conclude against precisely the estimators that work once extremes actually cluster.

Coverage is not the quantity to read this from, for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:simulation}: at \(\theta = 1\) the reference lies on the boundary of the admissible range, so every estimator's interval is distorted there and in opposite directions. Runs and K-gaps undercover, at \(0.40\) and \(0.39\) against a nominal \(0.95\), because an estimate at the boundary can only err downwards and a symmetric percentile interval misses systematically. Ferro--Segers and disjoint blocks attain \(1.000\), but with boundary-clipping probabilities between \(0.50\) and \(0.70\): their intervals contain the reference largely because the estimate saturates at \(1\), not because they are calibrated. Figure~\ref{fig:estimator-failure} reports the clipping share for this reason. Neither figure is evidence about interval quality, and no boundary-aware interval is implemented here that would make one.

Where the reference is interior the comparison is interpretable, and there runs and K-gaps cover at \(0.844\) and \(0.850\). That is the coverage evidence this study supports.

The no-declustering control behaves exactly as intended, with zero bias at \(\theta = 1\) and bias \(+0.500\) and \(+0.728\) in the two clustered rows, confirming that the comparison is measuring extremal clustering rather than an artifact of the harness.

\subsection{Alarm conversion and failed-detection regions}

The second research question asks whether declustering and alarm merging reduce duplicate alarms while preserving event recall. In the registered threshold grid, event recall was zero at all reported quantiles, with false-alarm burdens between 133.92 and 168.48 alarm episodes per operating day. This is a failed-detection region under the current observable, threshold grid, and matching rule; it shows that alarm conversion can destroy utility. Across all registered combinations of alarm merge gap and post-onset matching tolerance, event recall, precision, and \(F_1\) remained zero, and relaxing post-onset tolerance did not alter the result. Both grids are reported in the supplement rather than as main-text figures, because a zero-recall surface adds no scientific information beyond the statement that it is zero.

\subsection{Complete held-out event-level benchmark}
\label{sec:event-benchmark}

Nineteen methods were run on both compressor datasets under one shared alarm policy: identical warning horizon, matching tolerance, matching rule, operating exposure and independent unit, verified by the fairness audit in Supplementary Table~\ref{X-tab:supp-event-fairness}. Alarm merging is treated as part of the detector rather than the protocol, since four methods exist precisely to vary declustering; the audit reports which event conversions were used instead of requiring one. Supplementary Table~\ref{X-tab:supp-event-benchmark} reports the complete held-out event-level benchmark under the shared alarm policy, and Supplementary Table~\ref{X-tab:supp-event-provenance} records each method's score, threshold rule, event policy, merge gap, parameter count, calibration status and tuning partition.

\input{../reports/paper/latex/event_benchmark_compact.tex}

Five conclusions follow, and they are not uniform.

\emph{Alarm burden is the binding constraint for almost every method.} Of the eighteen dataset-method pairs that detect a held-out failure, seventeen do so at event precision below \(0.3\%\) and negative event utility. A maintenance function receiving those alarms would be responding overwhelmingly to false positives, and the layered decomposition in Section~\ref{sec:decomposition} places twenty-five of the thirty-eight rows at this stage rather than earlier.

\emph{The registered recurrence score adds no measurable event-level value.} It raises 2{,}770 episodes on MetroPT and 893 on MetroPT2, at 110.9 and 54.0 false alarms per operating day and event precision \(0.00036\) and \(0.0011\). Simpler policies beat it on both datasets. Its one distinguishing property is exposure: at \(0.13\%\) and \(0.06\%\) of the test period under warning it is far less intrusive than the engineering threshold, which is a real difference but not the early-warning claim the method was proposed to support.

\emph{A target region, not a simple threshold, is the best method on each dataset.} On MetroPT the rare-state region reaches 35.6 false alarms per operating day against 90.9 for the best single-sensor threshold, with more than double its precision. On MetroPT2 the failure-prototype region reaches \(0.12\) false alarms per day against \(21.2\). This reverses the ordering reported in an earlier version of this work, which was produced under the scaling fault described in Section~\ref{sec:method}: the unbounded denominator suppressed the region scores specifically, because a region distance aggregates every feature while a single-sensor threshold does not.

\emph{One configuration reaches an operationally plausible alarm rate.} On MetroPT2 the failure-prototype region raises three alarm episodes across 16.5 operating days, matches the held-out failure with a lead time inside the declared horizon, and attains event precision \(0.333\) at \(0.12\) false alarms per operating day. It is the only method in the benchmark with positive event utility, at \(+0.6\). No other row in this study is close to it, and it is treated separately in Section~\ref{sec:plausible-rate} because the qualifications it carries are as important as the number.

\emph{Classical EVT and anomaly baselines do not change the picture.} The generalised Pareto return level, the three declustering policies, SPOT, the isolation forest, the change-point detector, both autoencoders and the conformal detector all sit within the same precision decade as the registered score. Making the tail model or the declustering rule more sophisticated does not recover operational value that alarm conversion has already destroyed.

Alarm burden must be read on two axes that can disagree. Episode counts measure how often an operator is interrupted; time under warning measures how long the system is held in an alarmed state. On MetroPT the engineering threshold raises fewer episodes than the registered score (1551 against 2631) while holding the system under warning for 75.1\% of the test period against the registered score's 0.12\%. A detector that merges its exceedances into few long alarms therefore looks better on episode counts and far worse on exposure. Because episode-level precision and recall are computed after merging, a permanently-on detector attains a recall and precision of one with no unmatched episodes to count as false alarms; exposure is the only reported quantity that exposes it. Every alarm-burden comparison in this paper is therefore reported with both axes, and the supplementary benchmark flags any method whose warning exposure exceeds half the test period.

\subsection{Cross-dataset target-region transfer}
\label{sec:transfer}

Transfer is tested by fitting a target region on one compressor dataset and applying it to the other, not by comparing the two datasets' own metrics. Four protocols are separated: \emph{direct}, with scaling, prototypes, threshold and distance metric all frozen from the source; \emph{threshold recalibration} on the destination validation split; \emph{scaling recalibration} on the destination training split with the source prototypes retained; and a \emph{destination refit}, which is the within-dataset reference that degradation is measured against. Table~\ref{tab:target-region-transferability} reports all four in both directions.

Compatibility is checked before any region crosses. The two datasets share an identical schema and a compatible sampling cadence, 1.000 against 0.991 seconds, and five of the seven candidate features are admissible. Two are not: \texttt{reservoir\_pressure} and \texttt{flowmeter} have effectively zero overlap between the datasets, with standardised median shifts of 122 and 503 interquartile ranges. Those two channels occupy disjoint ranges across the datasets and are excluded rather than silently mapped, so the transfer runs on the five compatible features.

Transfer is strongly asymmetric. Applying the MetroPT region directly to MetroPT2 reaches event precision \(0.0057\) at 10.5 false alarms per operating day, which is better than a region fitted on MetroPT2 itself (\(0.0022\) at 27.0); the degradation against the within-dataset refit is negative. The reverse direction fails: the MetroPT2 region applied directly to MetroPT occupies \(50.3\%\) of the test period at precision \(0.00033\), an alarm that is on more often than off. Recalibration does not act uniformly. It rescues the failing direction, lifting MetroPT2 to MetroPT from \(0.00033\) to \(0.00108\), and it degrades the working one, dropping MetroPT to MetroPT2 from \(0.0057\) to \(0.0012\).

Figure~\ref{fig:transfer-distances} shows why the two directions differ. In the working direction the destination's failure states lie closer to the transferred prototypes than its normal operation, which is the ordering a usable region requires. In the failing direction that ordering inverts: MetroPT's normal operation sits closer to the MetroPT2 prototypes than MetroPT's own failure does, so the frozen threshold admits ordinary operation first and the region is occupied half the time. The failure is therefore not a matter of threshold placement, and no recalibration of the threshold alone can repair it.

The feature restriction also explains the MetroPT non-detection reported above. Holding the threshold rule fixed and varying only the feature set, the five compatible features detect the held-out MetroPT failure with 815 alarm episodes and recall \(1.0\), while all seven produce zero alarms and zero region occupancy. The seven-feature run reproduces the benchmark exactly, so the two constructions agree and the difference is attributable to the feature set alone. Including the two channels with no cross-dataset distributional support suppresses detection entirely on MetroPT.

The consequence for the paper's central heterogeneity claim is a narrowing rather than a reversal. A recurrence region does carry information across these two compressors in one direction, and better than refitting locally. But the direction matters, recalibration can hurt as easily as help, and part of what previously looked like dataset heterogeneity was a feature-selection artifact. The two datasets still cannot be pooled as equivalent evidence, and one directional success across two failures is not a transferability result.

\subsection{Matched negative controls}

Table~\ref{X-tab:matched-negative-controls} compares the observed failure-prototype alarm burden with 500 deterministic matched-control draws for each control family and dataset. These controls preserve occupancy, exposure length, temporal shifting, rare-region structure, or block structure while breaking the specific relationship to the held-out failure. The comparison is descriptive because only two independent held-out compressor failures are available.

Burden alone is not a sufficient basis for that comparison. A region that raises no alarms attains a false-alarm rate of zero while missing the failure, so comparing burden without conditioning on detection rewards a detector for not detecting. Table~\ref{tab:detection-aware-controls} therefore reports the control population's detection rate on every row and repeats the comparison restricted to controls that detect, that match the observed recall, and that match the observed occupancy. Utility is the predeclared joint utility defined in Section~\ref{sec:event-benchmark}: detection dominates, false alarms are charged per operating day, warning exposure is charged separately, and a capped early-warning reward is paid only when the failure was detected. Component metrics remain reported separately in Table~\ref{X-tab:matched-negative-controls}.

The conditioning changes both results. On MetroPT the observed region missed the failure, and so did all 3000 draws across the six control families: the detection rate is zero throughout and no draw satisfies the detecting condition. Its zero alarm burden is therefore matched by every control and accompanied by no detection, which is not evidence of a favourable region.

On MetroPT2 the conditioning weakens the comparison without reversing it. Restricting to controls that also detect lowers the observed percentile in two of the six families, from \(0.986\) to \(0.794\) against prototype-source permutations and from \(0.986\) to \(0.741\) against occupancy-matched random regions. In the other four it is unchanged at \(1.000\), where the observed region exceeds every detecting draw.

Those two families are the informative ones, and the reason they move is worth stating. Both preserve the region's own structure while breaking its relation to the failure, so a region that is genuinely failure-proximal should beat them and a region that merely occupies an unusual part of the state space should not. The observed region remains above their medians after conditioning, at roughly the three-quarter point of the detecting draws, which is favourable but not decisive. Their detection rates are also low, \(0.068\) and \(0.054\), so the detecting subsets are small and the percentiles are correspondingly imprecise. The four families the region beats outright are those whose draws detect often --- \(87\%\) of regime-matched rare regions and all phase-randomised scores find the same failure --- where detecting carries little information.

An earlier version of this work reported the opposite: percentiles falling to \(0.279\) and \(0.265\), below the control medians. Those numbers were produced under the scaling fault of Section~\ref{sec:method}, which suppressed the observed region's utility while leaving many controls less affected. The direction of the conditioning effect is unchanged --- conditioning on detection always weakens the observed region's standing, because unconditioned comparisons reward a region for not detecting --- but its magnitude was an artifact, and the corrected comparison does not place the region below its structure-preserving controls.

The sensitivity sweep varies each declared weight by factors from \(0.25\) to \(4\). Pooled across families the observed percentile among detecting draws is \(0.988\) throughout, so the standing is not an artifact of the chosen weighting.


\subsection{Score-threshold-cluster-alarm decomposition}
\label{sec:decomposition}

Table~\ref{X-tab:score-alarm-decomposition} follows every method's evidence through the stages that separate a score from a matched alarm: test observations, threshold exceedances, extreme clusters, alarm episodes after merging, and alarm episodes that match a labelled failure. Two properties of the table govern how it should be read. The first is that the columns are not comparable across stages: observations and exceedances count samples, clusters count extreme clusters, and the remaining columns count alarm episodes, so the decline along a row is a change of unit as much as a reduction in number. The second is that the labelled failures are ground truth, not a stage the detector passes through. They enter only at the matching step, which is why they are reported separately rather than as the terminus of the flow.

Figure~\ref{fig:metric-collapse} draws that structure for four representative rows: the detector track as connected circles, the labelled failures as a separate square joined to the matched-alarm stage by the matching step alone.

The decomposition shows that the failure layer is method-specific rather than a single property of the datasets. Table~\ref{tab:root-cause} classifies each dataset-method pair by the earliest stage at which its evidence broke down, with the evidence, the competing explanation the evidence does not exclude, and a confidence level.

Three rows never produce an exceedance at all. The failure-prototype region on MetroPT and the empirical horizon risk on both datasets return zero exceedances, zero clusters, and zero episodes: no evidence survives the score and threshold layers, so no alarm policy could have recovered the failure. This is the layer at which the MetroPT non-detection reported above actually occurs.

Zero exceedances is consistent with two different faults, and the counts alone cannot separate them. Either the score does not rank failure-proximal states above normal ones, or it does and the threshold discarded that ranking. We separate them with the warning-window rank separation: the probability that a sample drawn from the declared horizon before onset outranks a sample drawn from the rest of the split, where 0.5 is chance. The distinction matters because the repairs differ: a score failure needs a different observable, a threshold failure needs recalibration on a score that already carries the signal.

Applied to every row, the separation shows that most methods do rank the pre-onset window above chance on both datasets. On MetroPT the registered recurrence score separates at \(0.78\) and on MetroPT2 at \(0.86\); seven of the nineteen MetroPT methods and two of the nineteen MetroPT2 methods fall below the \(0.55\) floor. The score layer is therefore not where most of these methods break.

This corrects an earlier version of this analysis, and the correction is worth stating because it illustrates what the layered view is for. Under a previous scaling of the observable the MetroPT separation was \(0.45\), below chance, and the result was reported as a score-layer failure. That scaling divided each feature by its training interquartile range with no lower bound. MetroPT's \texttt{pressure\_tp2} idles near zero, so its interquartile range is \(0.004\) against a range of about ten, and dividing by it multiplied ordinary variation by roughly two hundred and fifty. One channel then dominated the maximum, the \(98\)th-percentile threshold rose above two thousand, and the ordering the other channels carried was discarded before any threshold was applied. Section~\ref{sec:method} now floors the denominator at a fraction of each feature's own range. The layer diagnosis is only as good as the score construction it examines, and a separation below chance is as consistent with a preprocessing fault as with an uninformative observable.

With the corrected observable the failures concentrate after alarm conversion. Of the 38 dataset-method rows, 25 match the failure and fail on burden, nine fail at the score layer, two at event matching, one at the threshold, and one is recorded as insufficient independent evidence. The registered recurrence score reaches the failure on both datasets and raises 2{,}770 and 893 alarm episodes to do it, at event precision \(0.00036\) and \(0.0011\).

That burden makes recall almost uninformative on its own. The circular-shift null in Section~\ref{sec:method} preserves each detector's episode count, episode durations and the burstiness of their spacing, and randomises only its alignment to the failure. Under it the registered score matches with probability \(0.9997\) on MetroPT and \(0.9994\) on MetroPT2. A detector alarming this often is essentially certain to overlap a one-hour window wherever that window falls, so its recall of \(1.0\) is not evidence that it anticipated anything. The same null separates the sparse methods sharply: the SPOT-style baseline sits at \(0.034\) and \(0.040\), and the failure-prototype region on MetroPT2 at \(0.17\).

One row is unlike the rest. On MetroPT2 the failure-prototype region raises three alarm episodes across 16.5 operating days, matches the held-out failure, and reaches event precision \(0.333\) at \(0.12\) false alarms per operating day. It is the only method in the benchmark with positive event utility. Three considerations keep this suggestive rather than established. Its chance-match probability is \(0.17\), so roughly one in six detectors with this episode structure would match by alignment alone. Its three episodes hold the system under warning for \(16.4\%\) of the test period, which is the exposure asymmetry described above working against it: few long episodes flatter an episode-level precision that an operator would not recognise. And it rests on one held-out failure. Under the same construction on MetroPT the region raises no alarms at all.

The negative-control region is the check on this procedure. It separates at \(0.51\) on MetroPT and \(0.10\) on MetroPT2, matches nothing on either, and has chance-match probabilities of \(0.16\) and \(0.71\); a control built to carry no failure information is scored as carrying none.


\subsection{A configuration that reaches an operational alarm rate}
\label{sec:plausible-rate}

One row of the benchmark is separated from the rest by more than an order of magnitude, and this section states what it does and does not support.

On MetroPT2, the failure-prototype region raises three alarm episodes across the 16.5 operating days of the held-out test split. One of them matches the labelled failure inside the declared one-hour horizon. Event precision is \(0.333\), the false-alarm rate is \(0.12\) per operating day, and event utility is \(+0.6\), the only positive value in the benchmark. The next-best method on that dataset raises 354 episodes at 21.2 false alarms per day. Against the alarm-capacity constraint discussed in Section~\ref{sec:related}, one false alarm every eight days from a monitored asset is a rate a maintenance function could absorb, which is not true of anything else reported here.

Three qualifications bound what this establishes, and each is measured rather than asserted.

The first is coincidence. The circular-shift null gives this configuration a chance-match probability of \(0.17\): roughly one detector in six with this episode structure would intersect the warning window from alignment alone. That is far below the \(0.999\) of the busy detectors, so the match is not the artifact of volume that theirs are, but it is not a small probability either. On a single event it cannot be reduced further by any analysis of that event.

The second is exposure, and it cuts against the headline number. Those three episodes hold the system under warning for \(16.4\%\) of the test period, roughly twenty-one hours each. This is precisely the degeneracy Section~\ref{sec:method} describes: episode-level precision improves when a detector merges its evidence into few long alarms, and an operator experiences a detector that is on for a sixth of the record rather than one that interrupts three times. By the exposure axis this configuration is worse than the registered score, which is under warning for \(0.06\%\) of the same split. Which axis matters depends on whether an alarm triggers an inspection or a persistent indication, and the two rank these methods oppositely.

The third is that it rests on one failure, on one dataset, under one feature construction. The same region built the same way on MetroPT raises no alarms at all and misses that failure entirely, and Section~\ref{sec:transfer} shows that the difference is attributable to the feature set rather than to the method. A configuration that succeeds on one of two available events and fails on the other is not a result about a method; it is a hypothesis about one.

We therefore report this as the finding most worth pursuing rather than as a demonstration that recurrence regions work. What would settle it is specific: more independent failure episodes on the same asset class, the region frozen prospectively before those events occur, and an exposure budget declared in advance alongside the episode budget so the two axes cannot be traded after the fact.

\subsection{Method-specific failure layers}
\label{sec:root-cause}

Table~\ref{tab:root-cause} states the failure layer for each dataset and method, together with the evidence, the competing explanation that evidence does not exclude, and a confidence level. It covers the registered recurrence score, the engineering threshold, the classical peaks-over-threshold fit, Isolation Forest, both target regions, and the best-performing baseline outside those families, selected on event utility from the benchmark rather than named in advance.

Two properties of this table are the point of it. The first is that a dataset does not have a failure layer; a dataset-method pair does. On MetroPT the failure-prototype region and the registered recurrence score fail at opposite ends of the pipeline, the first before any alarm exists and the second only after alarm conversion, and a single explanation attached to MetroPT would be wrong for one of them. The second is that confidence varies with what the evidence can support. A zero-exceedance verdict is structural and rests on nothing but counts; a burden verdict is medium confidence because a different threshold or alarm policy is not excluded; and a method that matches with acceptable precision is recorded as insufficient independent evidence at low confidence, because one held-out failure cannot distinguish a working detector from a fortunate one.

The competing-explanation column is deliberately populated for every row. None of these verdicts is a causal proof, and for the non-event datasets the entry states explicitly that an estimand mismatch is not evidence that the method fails on those units.

\subsection{Timeline reconciliation}

Figures~\ref{fig:timeline-metropt} and~\ref{fig:timeline-metropt2} show each held-out failure at two scales. The upper panels cover the full test split, so every alarm episode the method raised is visible at once; the lower panels expand the failure window itself. The two scales are shown together because either alone misleads. The local window around the MetroPT failure contains 23 alarm episodes, one of which matches the failure with a lead time of 0.93 hours. Read on its own that window describes a usable detector. MetroPT2 behaves the same way: 19 episodes in the plotted window against 852 over 16.5 days, 848 of them false, at 51.5 false alarms per operating day. The local view is a correct picture of the moment of detection and a misleading picture of the method.

The local windows also show that proximity is not usefulness. \subsection{External estimands}

SCANIA Component X supports a limitation the compressor datasets cannot express. Its independent unit is a vehicle and its label is a component repair, so the quantity a detector would estimate there is fleet-level repair risk rather than the onset of one machine's failure episode. Under the conservative high-quantile diagnostic it attains \(F_1 = 0\), and that number is uninformative about the method: no alarm policy on vehicle-level repair risk answers the question the held-out compressor events answer. Table~\ref{tab:root-cause} therefore records an estimand mismatch, with the competing explanation stating that this is not evidence the method fails on those units.

Hydraulic Systems and SECOM carry the same mismatch for laboratory cycle states and wafer yield labels respectively. All three are reported in Supplementary Section~\ref{X-supp:realdata} rather than the main text, because a stress test whose estimand differs from the primary one cannot strengthen or weaken the primary claim.
