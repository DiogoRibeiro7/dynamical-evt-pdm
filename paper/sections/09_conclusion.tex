\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study demonstrated a reproducible way to test whether clustered extremes become early warnings, and the answer on the evaluated public data is negative. What is supported is the diagnostic apparatus: the Monte Carlo study establishes where the extremal-index estimators are usable and where their ranking inverts, and the decomposition identifies which stage of each method destroys the signal. What is not supported is early warning. The registered recurrence score matched the two held-out compressor failures, but only with 2631 and 852 alarm episodes and false-alarm burdens of 105.34 and 51.47 alarms per operating day, one to two orders of magnitude above what alarm-management practice treats as absorbable. On MetroPT the match is not even evidence of detection: the score ranks the pre-onset window below chance, and a detector alarming that often matches by coincidence with probability 0.99. Nor is the comparison against simple policies settled by episode counts alone: the engineering threshold raises fewer episodes while keeping the system under warning for most of the MetroPT test period, so alarm burden is only interpretable when interruption frequency and warning exposure are reported together.

Target regions do transfer, but directionally and only on features that support it. Two of the seven candidate channels have no distributional overlap between the datasets; on the remaining five, a region fitted on MetroPT outperforms on MetroPT2 a region fitted on MetroPT2 itself, while the reverse direction admits ordinary operation half the time. Recalibration rescues the failing direction and degrades the working one, so transfer cannot be reported as a single number. The same feature restriction, not the method, explains the region's MetroPT non-detection.

The observed MetroPT2 region survives conditioning on detection, which is a weaker statement than it sounds. It exceeds every detecting draw in four of the six control families, and sits at the \(0.79\) and \(0.74\) percentiles of the two families that preserve its structure while breaking its relation to the failure. Those two are the informative comparisons and they are also the least precise, since only about one draw in fifteen detects at all. Conditioning lowers the observed standing in exactly those two families, which is the direction to expect, and an earlier version of this work reported them falling below the control medians; that reversal was an artifact of the scaling fault and does not survive its correction. On MetroPT the region's zero burden is vacuous, since neither it nor any of the 3000 control draws detected the failure. One configuration, the MetroPT2 failure-prototype region, reaches an alarm rate a maintenance function could absorb; on a single event, at a chance-match probability of \(0.17\) and holding the system under warning for a sixth of the split, that is a hypothesis worth testing rather than a method shown to work.

The main failure mechanisms are separated per method rather than per dataset. Of the 38 dataset-method rows, 25 fail after alarm conversion, nine at the score layer, two at event matching and one at the threshold. For most methods on both datasets the observable ranks the pre-onset window above chance and the conversion to alarms is what removes the value, which is the layer a future method could plausibly address. The failure-prototype region on MetroPT remains an exception in the other direction, admitting no exceedance at all, and the SPOT-style baseline ranks the window correctly while raising its alarms elsewhere. Non-event datasets add stress-test evidence only; their cycles, vehicles, and wafers are not independent compressor failure episodes.

Target-region methods add diagnostic value by exposing heterogeneity between MetroPT and MetroPT2, but no evaluated method establishes reliable operational early warning. The evidence required next is specific: more independent failure episodes, prospectively frozen target regions, stable regime definitions, deployment-realistic alarm policies, and external validation showing acceptable recall, false-alarm burden, lead time, and utility under the same event-level protocol.
