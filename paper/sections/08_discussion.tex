\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Estimator finite-sample validity}

The Monte Carlo results support a bounded interpretation of the EVT diagnostics. Runs, Ferro--Segers, K-gaps, reciprocal mean cluster size, block clustering, and no-declustering references are useful for detecting estimator instability across process families, thresholds, missingness, and dynamical structure. They do not by themselves identify maintenance-relevant degradation. The practical implication is that recurrence estimates should be treated as conditional diagnostics tied to the chosen observable, threshold, run length, and sampling process.

\subsection{Target-region transferability}

The central empirical finding is directional. Under the seven-feature construction the failure-prototype region missed the held-out MetroPT failure while detecting the MetroPT2 failure with 157 alarm episodes and 9.41 false alarms per operating day, materially better than the registered recurrence score on MetroPT2. A genuine transfer experiment changes how that should be read. On the five schema-compatible features the region fitted on MetroPT transfers to MetroPT2 and outperforms a region fitted on MetroPT2 itself, while the reverse direction fails outright, and the original MetroPT non-detection turns out to be a property of including two channels whose distributions do not overlap between the datasets.

Two cautions follow. The first is that a recalibration step cannot be assumed benign: recalibrating the threshold or the scaling on the destination rescued the failing direction and degraded the working one, so recalibration should be reported as a separate experiment rather than folded into a single transfer number. The second is that a feature set chosen for within-dataset performance is not automatically a feature set that supports transfer, and here it actively prevented detection. The evidence supports directional and feature-dependent transfer, not a general target-region solution, and two held-out failures cannot settle the question either way.

\subsection{Score, threshold, clustering, and alarm-conversion failure}

The failure mode is layered, and the decomposition locates each method's layer rather than assigning one explanation to a dataset. Twenty-five of the 38 rows reach the failure and fail on alarm burden, nine fail at the score layer, two at event matching, one at the threshold, and one is not estimable. The concentration after alarm conversion is the substantive finding: for most of these methods the observable carries pre-onset ordering and the conversion from exceedances to alarms destroys its operational value.

The route to that finding is worth recording, because it bears on how much weight a layer diagnosis can carry. An earlier version of this work reported MetroPT as a score-layer failure on a separation of \(0.45\). That separation was produced by a scaling denominator with no lower bound, under which a channel that idles near zero acquired an interquartile range of \(0.004\) and dominated a maximum-absolute-\(z\) score. The diagnosis was correct about the layer in a narrow sense, since the score was indeed where the information was lost, but wrong about the cause and therefore about the repair: the fault was in preprocessing, not in the observable, and flooring the denominator recovers a separation of \(0.78\). A layered diagnosis inherits every assumption of the pipeline it inspects, and a score-layer verdict should be read as a prompt to audit score construction before concluding that an observable is uninformative.

What survives the correction is the alarm-conversion result. The registered score reaches both failures and needs thousands of episodes to do it, and the circular-shift null shows that a detector alarming that often matches essentially regardless of whether it anticipated anything. Recall at these rates is close to uninformative, which is why the null belongs beside it.

Two cautions. The separation is measured against one failure window per dataset, so it describes these events rather than estimating a population quantity, and the confidence column in Table~\ref{tab:root-cause} records that. And a low separation under the declared one-hour horizon does not exclude separation at a different lead time, which is the competing explanation the table names for every score-failure row.

Two cautions follow from reading the stages as a flow. The counts change unit at every stage, so a steep decline between exceedances and episodes reflects a change from samples to episodes as much as any reduction the method achieved, and comparing methods across stages is not meaningful. More importantly, the labelled failures are not the end of the detector's flow. Placing them there, as an earlier version of this figure did, invites reading the decomposition as a derivation from failures back to failures; they are a separate track that meets the detector only at the matching step, and the pipeline never uses them before that point.

\subsection{Comparison With Baselines}

Simple baselines remain competitive under the held-out event policy, in the weak sense that they fail in the same place. The engineering threshold matched both compressor events, but on MetroPT it does so with a separation of \(0.45\), which is the same score-layer failure as the registered method rather than a competitive detection. Its burden relative to the registered score depends on the axis measured: fewer alarm episodes, but far greater warning exposure. That asymmetry is itself the operational point. A method can be made to look better on episode counts by merging its alarms into fewer, longer blocks, which is not an improvement an operator would recognise, so claiming a burden advantage requires naming the burden. Classical EVT and anomaly baselines did not remove the basic precision problem. The proposed recurrence score therefore adds diagnostic structure, but it does not add measurable operational value on these held-out events.

\subsection{Matched Negative Controls}

Matched controls prevent over-interpreting one favourable target-region result, but only when they are conditioned on detection. Comparing alarm burden without that condition rewards a region for not detecting: a region raising no alarms attains the best possible burden and the worst possible outcome simultaneously. Both compressor results change once the condition is imposed.

On MetroPT the failure-prototype region raised no alarms and missed the failure, and so did every one of the 3000 control draws across six families. The comparison is therefore vacuous: a zero burden that is matched by every control and accompanied by no detection is not evidence of anything. On MetroPT2 the region detects, and conditioning lowers its standing against every control family. In two of the six it falls below the control median, and those two are the informative ones: occupancy-matched random regions and prototype-source permutations preserve the region's own structure while breaking its relation to the failure. The families the region still beats are those whose draws detect almost always, where detection separates nothing.

The honest reading is narrower than the earlier one. Against chance regions that share its structure and also detect, the learned region is not better; against controls that detect trivially it is. Part of what the unconditioned comparison measured was the difficulty of the detection problem rather than the quality of the region, and the residual advantage rests on control families that discriminate least.

\subsection{Independent-Event Scarcity}

Only two independent held-out compressor failures are available. This prevents stable estimation of event-level uncertainty, calibration, and failure-type heterogeneity. Timestamp counts cannot substitute for independent failures because alarm episodes are temporally dependent and can cluster around the same operating regime.

\subsection{Operational Implications}

The workflow is useful as an audit of how extreme-score methods fail. It exposes whether apparent score excursions survive thresholding, declustering, alarm merging, and event matching. It does not establish deployable early warning. Deployment would require prospectively validated target regions, enough independent failures or entities, acceptable false-alarm burden, useful lead time, and comparison against simple policies under the same episode-level evaluation.
