\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Industrial maintenance alarms are useful only when they map to the decision unit that operators and engineers actually face. A pointwise anomaly score may flag thousands of telemetry samples around one physical failure, while the operator sees one alarm burden, one repair decision, and one opportunity for useful warning. The same difficulty appears outside compressor telemetry: a fleet repair record, a laboratory hydraulic cycle, and a semiconductor yield label are not exchangeable evidence units. Any empirical claim must therefore separate samples, exceedances, clusters, alarm episodes, failures, vehicles, cycles, and production examples.

Dynamical extreme-value theory offers a tempting route from recurrent machine states to early-warning logic. If an observable is large when the trajectory returns near a target set, then recurrence and periodicity can cluster extremes \citep{Freitas2008Hitting,Freitas2012Periodicity}. In industrial monitoring, however, clustered extremes can also arise from operating regimes, preprocessing, smoothing, missingness, persistent shifts, or alarm-conversion rules. Noise can further weaken the connection between deterministic recurrence and observed extremes \citep{Faranda2013Noise}. The scientific question is therefore not whether an extremal-index diagnostic can be computed. The question is when such a diagnostic translates into useful episode-level warning.

This paper is framed as a negative-result study. It asks four research questions. First, does threshold and regime conditioning stabilise extremal-index diagnostics relative to global thresholding? Second, does declustering reduce duplicate alarm episodes without destroying event recall or warning lead time? Third, does the registered robust extreme-score diagnostic add operational value after episode conversion? Fourth, which mechanisms explain empirical failure: target-region misspecification, regime mixture, too few independent failures, non-extreme fault signatures, label ambiguity, calibration shift, or alarm conversion?

The evaluated workflow combines causal state construction, training-only target-region construction, regime-conditioned thresholds, extremal-index diagnostics, multivariate lag patterns, alarm merging, event matching, baseline provenance, and dataset-specific condition diagnostics. The interpretation is deliberately conservative so that negative and non-estimable outcomes remain visible.

The contribution is narrow. The study shows that dynamical-EVT-inspired diagnostics can reveal dependence, threshold sensitivity, duplicate-alarm behavior, and calibration limits, but the current public-data evidence does not show reliable early warning. MetroPT and MetroPT2 are the primary event-level case studies; SCANIA Component X is an external vehicle-level stress test; Hydraulic Systems and SECOM are supplementary condition-monitoring demonstrations. This separation is essential because the datasets support different estimands. The paper therefore reports what failed, where the evidence is insufficient, and which conditions would be required before making broader predictive-maintenance claims.
