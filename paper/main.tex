\begin{abstract}
Whether clustered extreme scores become useful maintenance alarms is a question about \emph{where} a detector fails, not only whether it does. This paper builds a layered evaluation that separates a detector into score, threshold, extreme clusters, alarm episodes and matched events, and applies it to public industrial monitoring data. A Monte Carlo study over fourteen process families at 500 replications per cell shows the ranking of six extremal-index estimators inverting between regimes: runs and K-gaps carry the largest bias of the six for an independent process and the smallest once extremes genuinely cluster, so a reader seeing only near-independent processes would reject the estimators that work. Interval coverage is not used to make that case, because an independent process places the reference on the boundary of the admissible range, where every estimator's interval is distorted and two of them attain high coverage by saturating rather than by calibration. Nineteen methods are then evaluated on two independent held-out compressor failures under one shared alarm policy. Measuring how well each score ranks the hour before onset against the rest of the test split, most separate well above chance on both datasets, and 25 of the 38 dataset-method rows fail only after alarm conversion: the registered recurrence score matches both failures, at 2770 and 893 alarm episodes and event precision below \(0.12\%\). A circular-shift null, which preserves each detector's episode structure while breaking its alignment to the failure, shows that detectors alarming this often match with probability above \(0.99\) regardless, so recall alone carries almost no information at these rates. Against that background one configuration stands apart by more than an order of magnitude: on MetroPT2 the failure-prototype region raises 3 alarm episodes across 16.5 operating days, matches the failure, and reaches event precision \(0.333\) at \(0.12\) false alarms per operating day, the only positive event utility in the benchmark, where the next-best method raises 354 episodes. Its qualifications are measured rather than asserted. Its chance-match probability is \(0.17\); its three episodes hold the system under warning for \(16.4\%\) of the split, which is worse than the registered score on that axis and is the merging degeneracy the evaluation is built to expose; and the same construction on MetroPT raises no alarms at all, a difference attributable to the feature set. Target regions transfer directionally and only on the five of seven features with cross-dataset distributional support. On two failures, no evaluated method is shown to deliver reliable early warning and none is shown to be incapable of it, and the configuration above is reported as the hypothesis most worth testing rather than as a result.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Code and Data Availability}

Code, configuration files, data-acquisition scripts, generated-paper tooling, and reproducibility materials are maintained in the project repository \citep{Ribeiro2026DynEvtPdmRepository}. It carries a semantic version tag, a BSD-3-Clause licence, \texttt{CITATION.cff} and \texttt{codemeta.json} metadata, release notes, and an artifact manifest recording the hash of every generated figure and table, so every number in this paper is traceable to the commit that produced it.

The repository is public, and the results in this paper correspond to the tagged release \texttt{v0.3.2}. A reader can retrieve that state, install the pinned environment from the lock file, and re-run the pipeline that produced every figure and table reported here.

That release is archived, and the software citation resolves to an immutable deposit rather than to a mutable host. The cited identifier is the concept DOI \href{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21803768}{10.5281/zenodo.21803768}, which names the software across all its versions. Every number reported in this paper was produced by release \texttt{v0.3.2}, and that version string is what pins the state rather than the DOI itself; readers who want an identifier for that snapshot alone can use its version DOI, \href{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21803769}{10.5281/zenodo.21803769}.

Citing the concept DOI is deliberate. A version DOI is minted from a snapshot after that snapshot is frozen, so an archive can never contain the identifier issued for it, and a paper pinned to one is pinned to an archive that does not describe itself. A concept DOI is fixed once and does not change, so it remains correct as the software continues, and naming the version in the text supplies the precision the identifier gives up.

Raw third-party datasets are not redistributed by this repository; the acquisition pipeline stores them locally under \texttt{data/raw/} and documents their public sources through the dataset registry.
